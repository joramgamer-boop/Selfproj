# Mission: The Sociological Imagination (C. Wright Mills)

## Why

Joram is taking an introductory sociology course where Mills' _The Sociological
Imagination_ (1959) is assigned reading, starting with Chapter 1, "The Promise."
This is their first sociology text. The goal is to be able to read dense
sociological primary sources without getting lost, and to use Mills' framework
accurately and confidently in written coursework and class discussion.

## Success looks like

- Taking any real situation — from the news, from their own life — and correctly
  splitting it into a **personal trouble** and a **public issue**, with reasons.
- Explaining what the sociological imagination is, aloud and from memory, in
  under a minute, without reciting a definition.
- Writing an essay or exam answer that quotes and uses Mills accurately, rather
  than paraphrasing a study-guide summary.
- Reading the remaining pages of the chapter and tracking the argument's
  structure — knowing at any point what Mills is doing and why.

## Constraints

- **First sociology text.** No prior vocabulary can be assumed. Terms like
  _milieu_, _social structure_, and _biography_ have to be built, not invoked.
- Coursework deadlines are external and unknown — lessons should stay short and
  self-contained so any single session is useful on its own.
- Mills' prose is 1959 and deliberately dense. Difficulty in the _reading_ is
  unavoidable; difficulty in the _lesson_ should be spent on practice, not on
  decoding.

## Out of scope

- Chapters 2–6 (Grand Theory, Abstracted Empiricism, and the rest of Mills'
  attack on mid-century sociology) until Chapter 1 is solid.
- The wider theory canon — Marx, Weber, Durkheim, Parsons — beyond the minimum
  needed to understand why Mills name-checks them.
- Research methods and statistics.
