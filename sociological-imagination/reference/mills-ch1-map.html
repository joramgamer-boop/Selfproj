Reference · The Sociological Imagination

Chapter 1: The Promise

A map of the argument, the vocabulary, and the quotable lines — C. Wright Mills, 1959, pages 3–24.

The thesis in one sentence

The sociological imagination enables us to grasp history and biography and the relations between the two within society. That is its task and its promise.

Mills, p. 6

Everything else in the chapter is either a defence of that sentence, a tool for carrying it out, or an attack on sociologists who have abandoned it.

On Mills' “men”. Mills writes in the generic masculine of 1959 — “ordinary men”, “his own fate”. Read it as “people”. When quoting in an essay, quote him exactly and let the language stand as evidence of the period; don't silently modernise it.

The six sections, and what each one does

§PagesJob it does
—3–5 The setup. People feel trapped. They are right to. What they lack is not information or reasoning skill but a quality of mind.
15–8 The definition. Names the sociological imagination and gives the three questions every classic analyst asks.
28–11 The tool. Personal troubles of milieu vs. public issues of social structure, worked through four examples.
311–13 The diagnosis. Ours is an age of uneasiness and indifference — feelings not yet formulated as troubles or issues.
413–18 The claim to the throne. Physical science and literature have failed as the “common denominator” of cultural life; social science is taking over.
518–22 The book's aim. Mills states his purpose, admits his biases, and promises to increase sociologists' unease.
622–24 The hit list. Three tendencies in sociology, each with its characteristic distortion — the targets of chapters 2–6.

Vocabulary

Mills builds these terms rather than borrowing them. Each definition below is his, compressed.

TermWhat it meansPage
Sociological imagination A quality of mind: the capacity to see one's own life as the meeting point of biography and history, and to shift freely between the two. Not a theory and not a discipline — a way of thinking. 5–7
Biography One person's life as it is actually lived — their career, their inner life, the shape of a single existence. 6
History The large-scale movement of a society over time: industrialisation, war, the rise and fall of classes. 3–4
Milieu (plural: milieux) “The social setting that is directly open to [a person's] personal experience and to some extent his willful activity” — job, family, neighbourhood. The close-up scenes of a life. 8
Social structure The organisation of many milieux into the institutions of a whole society, and the way those milieux “overlap and interpenetrate”. 8
Personal trouble “A private matter: values cherished by an individual are felt by him to be threatened.” Occurs within a person's character and their immediate relations. 8
Public issue “A public matter: some value cherished by publics is felt to be threatened.” Transcends the individual's local environment; often involves “a crisis in institutional arrangements”. 8–9
Common denominator The one style of thinking an age reaches for by default. Was Newtonian physics, then Darwinian biology; Mills argues it is becoming the sociological imagination. 13–14
Classic social analysis Mills' name for the tradition he is defending: “a definable and usable set of traditions” whose “essential feature is the concern with historical social structures”. 21

Trouble

Mills' four worked examples

These are the chapter's engine. Learn them and you can reproduce his method on any case.

CaseAs a troubleAs an issue
Unemployment One man out of work in a city of 100,000. Look to his character, his skills, his immediate opportunities. 15 million unemployed out of 50 million. “The very structure of opportunities has collapsed.”
War How to survive it, die in it with honour, profit from it, or help end it. Its causes; what types of men it throws into command; “the unorganized irresponsibility of a world of nation-states”.
Marriage A man and a woman experiencing difficulty inside one marriage. A divorce rate of 250 per 1,000 in the first four years — an indication of a structural issue in the institution itself.
The metropolis Solved privately by wealth: a flat in the centre, a house forty miles out, a helicopter between them. What should be done with the city itself — and “who is to decide and to accomplish whatever choice is made?”
The move that makes it work

Notice that each row is one phenomenon described at two levels — not two different problems. Mills' point is that a private solution can be perfectly real (the helicopter works) and still leave the issue untouched.

The three questions classic analysts ask

Mills' checklist, pp. 6–7. Useful as an essay skeleton.

1. Structure What is the structure of this society as a whole? What are its essential components, and how are they related?
2. Place in history Where does this society stand in human history? By what mechanics is it changing?
3. Human variety What varieties of men and women prevail in this society and period? How are they “selected and formed, liberated and repressed”?

The values-and-threat grid

Section 3's least-quoted, most useful passage (p. 11). Two questions — do you hold a cherished value, and do you feel it threatened — give four states.

No threat feltThreat felt
Values known Well-being Crisis — experienced as a trouble or an issue. If all values are involved: panic.
Values unclear Indifference — total, it becomes apathy. Uneasiness, anxiety — total, it becomes “a deadly unspecified malaise”.

Mills' diagnosis of his own era sits in the bottom row, and it produces the chapter's sharpest line:

It is the uneasiness itself that is the trouble; it is the indifference itself that is the issue.

Mills, p. 12

The three tendencies (§6) — a preview of the rest of the book

TendencyIts distortionNamed culprits
I — toward a theory of history A “trans-historical strait-jacket” forcing all of human history into one scheme, issuing gloomy prophecy Toynbee, Spengler
II — toward a systematic theory of man and society History abandoned; “an elaborate and arid formalism” where splitting Concepts becomes the whole endeavour — Grand Theory Parsons; formalists Simmel, Von Wiese
III — toward empirical studies of social facts Unrelated, insignificant facts of milieu; “methods have become Methodology” Lundberg, Stouffer, Dodd, Lazarsfeld

Lines worth quoting

p. 3 “Nowadays men often feel that their private lives are a series of traps.”
p. 3 “Neither the life of an individual nor the history of a society can be understood without understanding both.”
p. 5 “The personal uneasiness of individuals is focused upon explicit troubles and the indifference of publics is transformed into involvement with public issues.”
p. 6 “The sociological imagination enables us to grasp history and biography and the relations between the two within society.”
p. 8 “Perhaps the most fruitful distinction with which the sociological imagination works is between ‘the personal troubles of milieu’ and ‘the public issues of social structure.’”
p. 11 “To be aware of the idea of social structure and to use it with sensibility is to be capable of tracing such linkages among a great variety of milieux.”
p. 13 “‘Man's chief danger’ today lies in the unruly forces of contemporary society itself.”
A footnote worth reading (pp. 18–19). Mills admits he dislikes the phrase “social sciences”, would prefer “the human disciplines”, and concedes that “the political imagination” or “the anthropological imagination” would serve as well. He keeps “sociological” partly because “every cobbler thinks leather is the only thing”. Good evidence for an essay that Mills is describing a habit of mind, not staking out disciplinary turf.
