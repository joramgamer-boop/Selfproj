Lesson 01 · The Sociological Imagination

Troubles and Issues

Mills' sharpest tool, and the one move that makes it work. Fifteen minutes.

Chapter 1 is a manifesto, and manifestos are exhausting to read because everything in them sounds equally important. It isn't. Mills tells you himself which part matters most:

Perhaps the most fruitful distinction with which the sociological imagination works is between ‘the personal troubles of milieu’ and ‘the public issues of social structure.’ This distinction is an essential tool of the sociological imagination and a feature of all classic work in social science.

Mills, p. 8

That is your target for this lesson. Not the whole chapter — this one distinction, well enough to use it on cases Mills never saw.

The two definitions

Mills gives them on a single page. Read them as his, not as a textbook's: the phrasing is doing work.

Trouble
Occurs “within the character of the individual and within the range of his immediate relations with others.” It is stated and solved within that person's life and their immediate milieu — the setting directly open to their own experience and, to some degree, their own will.
“A trouble is a private matter: values cherished by an individual are felt by him to be threatened.”
Issue
Concerns “matters that transcend these local environments.” It has to do with how many milieux are organised into the institutions of a whole society — the social structure — and often involves “a crisis in institutional arrangements.”
“An issue is a public matter: some value cherished by publics is felt to be threatened.”
Milieu (French, mee-LYUH; plural milieux) is the load-bearing word here. Mills means the close-up scenes of a life: job, family, neighbourhood. Full vocabulary in the chapter map.

The test Mills actually applies

Read those definitions and you might reach for the obvious test: is it happening to one person or to lots of people? That's close, but it is not what Mills does. Watch him:

When, in a city of 100,000, only one man is unemployed, that is his personal trouble, and for its relief we properly look to the character of the man, his skills, and his immediate opportunities. But when in a nation of 50 million employees, 15 million men are unemployed, that is an issue, and we may not hope to find its solution within the range of opportunities open to any one individual. The very structure of opportunities has collapsed.

Mills, p. 9

The numbers are not the test. They are evidence for the test. The test is the last sentence: where does the solution have to live? If a change in this person's character, skills or immediate circumstances could genuinely resolve it, it is a trouble. If no rearrangement available to any one individual could resolve it — because the arrangement itself is what's broken — it is an issue.

The move that trips people up

Troubles and issues are not two different problems. They are the same phenomenon described at two levels. Unemployment is both. Marriage is both. Mills' four examples are four rows of a table, not eight separate topics.

This is why his metropolis example matters: the rich family with a flat in the centre, a house forty miles out, and a helicopter between them has genuinely solved their troubles. The private solution works. And it leaves the issue entirely untouched (p. 10).

Practice: which level is this?

Some are Mills' own cases; some he never saw. Answer before you scroll back up — getting it wrong and then seeing why is worth more than getting it right by looking.

The harder half

Spotting the level is the easy skill. The one your essays will be graded on is the second move: given a trouble, name the issue behind it without sliding into either of the two traps.

Trap one is restating the trouble as advice — a fix that still lives inside the person's milieu. Trap two is subtler and much more common: making a claim about many individuals' characters and mistaking it for a claim about structure. “People these days lack discipline” is not a structural statement. It is a personal one, multiplied. A structural statement names an institutional arrangement.

Say it without looking

This is the part that builds retention rather than the comfortable feeling of having understood something. Write it out before you reveal anything.
