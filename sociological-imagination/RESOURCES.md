# The Sociological Imagination — Resources

## Knowledge

- **Book (primary source): _The Sociological Imagination_ — C. Wright Mills, Oxford University Press, 1959 (40th anniversary edition, 2000, with an afterword by Todd Gitlin).**
  [Publisher page](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-sociological-imagination-9780195133738)
  The text itself. Joram has a PDF of Chapter 1, "The Promise" (pp. 3–24).
  This is the highest-trust source available and should be quoted directly in
  any coursework. Use for: everything. Every claim in a lesson should be
  traceable to a page number here.

- **Chapter 1, "The Promise" — section map**
  The chapter has six numbered sections. Worth knowing because Mills' argument
  changes job at each break:
  §1 (pp. 5–8) defines the sociological imagination · §2 (pp. 8–11) the
  troubles/issues distinction · §3 (pp. 11–13) diagnoses the present as an age
  of "uneasiness and indifference" · §4 (pp. 13–18) argues social science is
  replacing physical science and literature as the common denominator of
  cultural life · §5 (pp. 18–22) states the book's aim · §6 (pp. 22–24) sketches
  the three tendencies in sociology the rest of the book will attack.

- **[Wikipedia: _The Sociological Imagination_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sociological_Imagination)**
  Orientation only, not a citable source. Useful for one fact worth knowing:
  in 1998 the International Sociological Association named it the **second most
  important sociological book of the 20th century**. Use for: placing the book
  in context before reading; never for the argument itself.

- **[Wikipedia: C. Wright Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._Wright_Mills)**
  Biography. Mills (1916–1962) was a Columbia sociologist and an outsider to the
  discipline's mainstream — which is exactly why Chapter 1 reads like a
  manifesto. Use for: understanding the chapter's combative tone.
